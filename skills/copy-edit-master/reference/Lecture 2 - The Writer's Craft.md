## McCloskey's Core Insight

**Writing is thinking made visible.**

Good academic writing isn't about following rules—it's about creating **intellectual rhythm** that guides your reader through your argument.

The goal: **Make complex ideas seem simple, not the other way around.**

# Part 1: The Music of Prose

---

## Rule #1: Writing Is Thinking

**"Writing resembles mathematics. Mathematics is a language, an instrument of communication. But so too language is a mathematics, an instrument of thought."**

Your prose should reveal the **logical structure** of your thinking.

---

## Choose Simple, Direct Words

### 背景知识：英语词汇的两大来源

**理解这一点，你才能写出有力量的英文**

---

## 英语词汇的历史分层

**两大词源系统：**

1. **盎格鲁-撒克逊词 (Anglo-Saxon)**
   - 来源：古英语（日耳曼语族）
   - 特点：英语的"本土"词汇
   - 用于：日常生活、基本概念

2. **拉丁词 (Latinate)**
   - 来源：拉丁语（经由法语）
   - 进入时间：1066年诺曼征服后
   - 用于：政府、法律、学术、宗教

---

## Why This Matters for Academic Writing

**常见误区：**
❌ 复杂的词 = 显得有学问
❌ 拉丁词 = 更"学术"

**真相：**
✓ 简单的词更清晰、更有力
✓ 好的学术写作是让复杂的想法变简单，不是反过来

---

## McCloskey's Principle: Be Concrete

**"Show, don't just tell."**

Instead of: "The intervention had a substantial effect."

Write: "Personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15% (p < 0.01)."

---

## The Power of Active Verbs

**❌ Weak (passive)**
> It was found that there was a significant correlation between the two variables.

**✓ Strong (active)**
> Income predicts consumption (β = 0.42, p < 0.01).

---

## Maintain Consistent Tense

**The golden rule: Stay in one tense throughout your paper.**

Switching tenses confuses readers and disrupts the flow of your argument.

---

## Use Ordinary Present Tense in Academic Writing

**Academic writing convention: Use simple present tense to report findings.**

> We find that personal feedback reduces overconfidence.
>
> The model predicts inflation with 85% accuracy.
>
> Table 2 shows the regression results.

---

## Why Present Tense?

**Present tense makes your findings timeless and authoritative.**

- Your research creates knowledge that **exists now**
- Findings are **general truths**, not just historical events
- Present tense is more direct and confident

---

## Common Tense Errors to Avoid

**❌ Mixing tenses unnecessarily:**
> We collected data from 311 subjects. The experiment shows that personal feedback reduces overconfidence. We found that group feedback had no effect.

**✓ Consistent present tense:**
> We collect data from 311 subjects. The experiment shows that personal feedback reduces overconfidence. We find that group feedback has no effect.

---

## When to Use Past Tense

**Use past tense only for specific historical events or procedures already completed:**

> Smith (2019) found similar results. *(describes what Smith did)*
>
> Before the experiment, we recruited subjects through campus advertisements. *(describes completed recruitment)*

**But for your own findings, use present:**

> We find that feedback reduces overconfidence.

---

## Choose Your Research Subject—and Stick With It

**Three options:**

1. **"We"** - Most common, inclusive, collaborative tone
2. **"I"** - Single-authored papers, personal perspective
3. **"The authors"** or **passive voice** - More formal, less common

**The key: Be consistent throughout your entire paper.**

---

## Research Subject: Examples

**✓ Using "We" consistently:**
> We design an experiment to test two interventions. We collect data from 311 subjects. We find that personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15%.

**✓ Using "I" consistently (single author):**
> I design an experiment to test two interventions. I collect data from 311 subjects. I find that personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15%.

---

## Don't Mix Subjects

**❌ Inconsistent subject switching:**
> We designed the experiment. The author collected the data. I analyze the results using probit regression.

**✓ Consistent:**
> We design the experiment. We collect the data. We analyze the results using probit regression.

---

## Recommendation: Use "We"

**"We" is preferred in modern academic writing because:**

- More engaging and direct
- Acknowledges collaborative nature of research
- Less stuffy than "the authors"
- Works for both single and multiple authors

**Even if you're writing alone, "we" includes the reader in your intellectual journey.**

---

## Create Rhythm Through Sentence Length

**Vary your sentences to create intellectual music:**

> Overconfidence—the tendency to overestimate one's abilities relative to others—affects hiring, trading, and corporate strategy.

> We test two interventions to reduce it.

> Personal feedback works; group feedback does not.

---

## Put Emphatic Words at the End

**The end carries the most weight.**

**❌ Weak ending**
> The data show a positive correlation, in most cases.

**✓ Strong ending**
> In most cases, the data show a positive correlation.

---

## Your Turn: Creating Rhythm

**Before:**
> The implementation of the new policy was undertaken by the management team in order to facilitate the enhancement of employee productivity.

**After:**
> Management implements the new policy to improve employee productivity.

# Part 2: The Architecture of Argument

---

## McCloskey's Rule: A Paragraph Should Have a Point

**"The paragraph should be a more or less complete discussion of one topic."**

Each paragraph = **one unit of thought**

---

## The Four Elements of a Strong Paragraph

1. **The Topic Sentence** - States the paragraph's central idea
2. **Logical Flow** - "Transitive writing" - each sentence builds on the last
3. **Evidence** - Support with data, examples, reasoning
4. **The Climax** - Most important point at the end

---

## Element 1: The Topic Sentence

**Makes a promise to the reader.**

> Personal feedback reduces overconfidence more effectively than group feedback.

The rest of the paragraph **must deliver** on that promise.

---

## Element 2: Logical Flow (Transitive Writing)

**Each sentence must connect to the one before it.**

Use:
- Transitions (however, therefore, moreover)
- Repeated key terms
- Logical progression

---

## Example: No Flow vs. Transitive Writing

**❌ Disconnected:**
> Personal feedback reduces overconfidence. Group norms affect behavior. We test 311 subjects. Overconfidence is common.

**✓ Connected:**
> Personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15%. This reduction occurs because personalized information forces individuals to confront evidence about their performance.

---

## Element 3: Evidence and Development

After stating your claim, **support it** with:
- Data
- Examples
- Logical reasoning
- Citations

---

## Element 4: The Climax

**Place your most important point at the end.**

**❌ Weak ending:**
> Personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15% (p < 0.01), though group feedback has no significant effect. We measure overconfidence as the difference between estimated and actual percentile rank.

**✓ Strong ending:**
> We measure overconfidence as the difference between estimated and actual percentile rank. Personal feedback reduces this gap by 15% (p < 0.01), while group feedback has no significant effect.

---

## McCloskey's Rule: Make Your Writing Cohere

**"Make writing hang together. The reader can understand writing that hangs together."**

This applies at every level:
- Words connect to form sentences
- Sentences connect to form paragraphs
- Paragraphs connect to form arguments

---

# Part 3: The Drama of Discovery

---

## Think of Your Paper as a Story

**A good paper has dramatic arc:**

1. **Setup** (Introduction) - What's the puzzle?
2. **Rising action** (Body) - Building momentum
3. **Climax** (Results) - The payoff
4. **Resolution** (Conclusion) - What does it mean?

---

## The Introduction: Three Tasks

1. **Motivate** the research question
2. **State** your contribution clearly
3. **Preview** the structure

---

## Task 1: Motivate the Question

**Don't just describe what you do. Explain WHY the reader should care.**

**❌ Weak:**
> Overconfidence is a cognitive bias. This paper studies overconfidence.

**✓ Strong:**
> Overconfidence affects hiring, trading, and corporate strategy. Despite its economic importance, we know little about how to reduce it.

---

## Task 2: State Your Contribution Clearly

**Be direct. Don't hide your findings.**

**Formula:** We do X. We find Y.

**❌ Vague:**
> This paper investigates various interventions that might potentially reduce overconfidence.

**✓ Clear:**
> We test two interventions with 311 subjects. Personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15%; group feedback has no effect.

---

## The Body: Building Momentum

**Each section should logically follow from the previous one.**

Use **descriptive headings** that tell a story:
- ❌ "Methodology"
- ✓ "Experimental Design: Testing Personal vs. Group Feedback"

---

## The Conclusion: Synthesize, Don't Repeat

**Don't just rehash the introduction.**

The conclusion should:
1. **Synthesize** findings - What do they mean together?
2. (Optional) **Acknowledge limitations**
3. (Optional) **Point to future work**

---

## Example: Strong Conclusion

> Overconfidence persists not because people lack information, but because they lack the *right* information. Aggregate statistics are easy to dismiss; personal feedback is not. Our findings suggest that organizations should invest in personalized feedback systems rather than providing aggregate statistics.

# Part 4: The Art of Rewriting

---

## McCloskey's Truth: Writing Is Rewriting

**"No one writes a perfect first draft. Good writing is rewriting."**

**Samuel Johnson:** "What is written without effort is in general read without pleasure."

---

## Three-Stage Revision Process

### Stage 1: Structural Edit
- Does the argument make sense?
- Is the structure logical?
- Do sections flow?

### Stage 2: Line Edit
- Apply all McCloskey's rules
- Hunt for bad words
- Create rhythm

### Stage 3: Proofread: Grammar, spelling, punctuation; Final polish

---

## Hunt and Destroy: McCloskey's Bad Words

**Words to eliminate:**
- concept → idea/notion/thought
- individuals → people
- situation → position/condition
- utilize → use
- facilitate → help
- process of → remove entirely
- this/these → the/such a

---

## The Order Around Switch Until It Good Sounds

**McCloskey's practical advice:**

> Listen for sentences that are monotonously long; listen for straggling sentences.

**If it sounds wrong, it probably is.**

---

## Example: Full Revision

**Before:**
> It is the purpose of this research to embark on an investigation of the intricate determinants that facilitate student success. We meticulously collect a comprehensive dataset. Our analysis utilizes sophisticated methodology and seems to suggest that study time is important.

**After:**
> This research examines what drives student success. We collect data from course records and time logs. Study time predicts performance (β = 0.34, p < 0.01).

---

## McCloskey's Golden Rule

**"Read your work aloud."**

Your ear will catch:
- Awkward phrasing
- Clumsy rhythms
- Unclear sentences
- Monotonous structure

---

# Summary: Three Levels of Impact

**Sentence level:**
- Active verbs, concrete words, varied length
- Cut the "bad words"
- Create rhythm

**Paragraph level:**
- One idea per paragraph
- Topic sentence promises, paragraph delivers
- Strong ending with key finding

**Paper level:**
- Motivate the question
- Build dramatic momentum
- Synthesize findings

---

## Remember McCloskey's Core Message

**"Writing is thinking."**

Clear writing reflects clear thinking.

**First,** write to discover what you think.

**Then,** revise to make it impossible for your reader to misunderstand you.

---

# The Ultimate Goal

**Good academic writing has style and substance.**

It creates **intellectual rhythm** that guides your reader through complex ideas with clarity and impact.

**Write to express, not to impress.**
