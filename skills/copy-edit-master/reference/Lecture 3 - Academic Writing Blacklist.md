# Lecture 3: The Academic Writing Blacklist

**Words to Strike from Your Vocabulary**

李学恒，中山大学岭南学院，2025年11月18日

---

## Good Writing = Knowing What NOT to Write

Certain words and phrases are common in academic writing.

**But they weaken your prose.**

They signal lazy thinking.

**Avoid them.**

---

## The Blacklist: Four Categories

1. **Over-selling/Hyperbolic Words**
2. **Empty/Meaningless Modifiers**
3. **Vague/Ambiguous Terms**
4. **ChatGPT-like Academic Fluff**

---

# Category 1: Over-selling/Hyperbolic Words

---

## The Problem

You are a **scholar**, not a **salesperson**.

Your job is to present evidence, not to hype your work.

**Let your results speak for themselves.**

---

## Blacklist: Over-selling Words

- novel / innovative
- vital / critical / crucial
- essential / indispensable
- revolutionary / groundbreaking
- unprecedented / unique
- profound / deep
- seminal / landmark
- paradigm-shifting
- state-of-the-art
- cutting-edge

---

## Example: "Novel"

**❌ Bad**
> This study presents a novel model of decision-making.


**✓ Good**
> This study presents a model of decision-making.


**Why?**
If your finding is new, the reader will know because it's not in the literature. You don't need to call it "novel."

---

## Example: "Novel/Innovative"

**❌ Bad**
> This study embarks on an innovative exploration into the underlying dynamics of overconfidence.


**✓ Good**
> This study examines two interventions to reduce overconfidence.

---

## Example: "Vital/Critical"

**❌ Bad**
> The insights gleaned from this study enrich the current discourse on cognitive bias, offering vital contributions.


**✓ Good**
> This study adds two findings to the overconfidence literature.

---

## Example: "Profound"

**❌ Bad**
> The implications for management practice and policy are profound.


**✓ Good**
> These findings suggest managers could use personalized feedback to improve decision-making.

---

## Example: "Revolutionary/Groundbreaking"

**❌ Bad**
> Our research holds the potential to revolutionize practices in economics, management, and public policy.


**✓ Good**
> These results may inform feedback policies in organizations.

---

## The Principle

**Show, don't tell.**

Don't claim your work is important.

**Demonstrate it with evidence.**

---

# Category 2: Empty/Meaningless Modifiers

---

## The Problem

These words are often used as **empty praise**.

Instead of telling the reader your analysis is "robust," **show them**.

Describe your:
- Methods
- Data
- Specification tests

---

## Blacklist: Empty Modifiers

- intricate / sophisticated (without explanation)
- comprehensive / extensive
- substantial / significant (without quantification)
- robust / rigorous (as empty adjectives)
- nuanced / subtle (without specificity)
- rich / fertile
- ample / abundant
- myriad

---

## Example: "Intricate"

**❌ Bad**
> We hereby present an intricate model that is designed to scrutinize the hypothesis.


**✓ Good**
> We present a model with two belief components.

---

## Example: "Comprehensive"

**❌ Bad**
> Through a comprehensive lab experiment, we scrutinize two targeted interventions.


**✓ Good**
> In a lab experiment with 311 subjects, we test two interventions.

---

## Example: "Substantial"

**❌ Bad**
> Overconfidence has garnered substantial attention from economists.


**✓ Good**
> Studies on overconfidence include work on hiring (Kausel et al., 2016) and trading (Barber & Odean, 2001).

---

## Example: "Robust"

**❌ Bad**
> Our robust analysis shows a significant effect.


**✓ Good**
> Using probit regression, we find that PPP reduces overconfidence (p < 0.01).

---

## Example: "Nuanced"

**❌ Bad**
> The relationship between feedback and confidence is nuanced.


**✓ Good**
> Personal feedback reduces overconfidence by 15%, while group feedback has no effect.

---

## The Principle

**Replace empty adjectives with specific details.**

- Not "comprehensive" → Tell me: 311 subjects, 3 treatments, 4 weeks
- Not "robust" → Tell me: probit regression, fixed effects, p < 0.01
- Not "substantial" → Tell me: 15% reduction, 2.2 percentage points

---

# Category 3: Vague/Ambiguous Terms

---

## The Problem

These words are **imprecise**.

- How many is "several"?
- What makes a finding "interesting"?

**Be specific.**

---

## Blacklist: Vague Terms

- various / numerous (be specific about quantity)
- several / multiple (give exact numbers)
- considerable / substantial
- relevant / appropriate
- interesting / intriguing
- important / significant (without context)
- complex / complicated (explain the complexity)
- advanced / sophisticated

---

## Example: "Various/Numerous"

**❌ Bad**
> Overconfidence affects various areas of the economy.


**✓ Good**
> Overconfidence affects hiring, trading, and corporate strategy.

---

## Example: "Several"

**❌ Bad**
> We recruited several hundred participants.


**✓ Good**
> We recruited 311 participants.

---

## Example: "Interesting"

**❌ Bad**
> We found interesting gender differences in responses.


**✓ Good**
> Men reduced estimates by 2.2% while women showed no significant change (p = 0.061).

---

## Example: "Important"

**❌ Bad**
> This variable plays an important role in the model.


**✓ Good**
> A one-unit increase in this variable raises the probability by 12 percentage points.

---

## The Principle

**Replace vague words with precise information.**

- Not "various" → List them: hiring, trading, strategy
- Not "several" → Count them: 311 participants
- Not "interesting" → Quantify it: men -2.2%, women 0%

---

# Category 4: ChatGPT-like Academic Fluff

---

## The Problem

This is the language of someone trying to **sound "academic."**

It is:
- Wordy
- Clichéd
- Empty

**Write directly instead.**

---

## Blacklist: Academic Fluff

- "This study embarks on..." → "This study examines..."
- "Paves the way for" → "enables"
- "Shed light on" → "show"
- "Garner attention" → "attract interest"
- "It is pivotal to understand" → "Note that"
- "Render it essential" → "make it important"

---

## Example: "Embarks on"

**❌ Bad**
> This paper embarks on an innovative exploration.


**✓ Good**
> This paper tests whether feedback reduces overconfidence.

---

## Example: "Paves the way"

**❌ Bad**
> Our analysis paves the way for more precise policy-making.


**✓ Good**
> These results suggest that organizations could use personalized feedback.

---

## Example: "Garner attention"

**❌ Bad**
> The ubiquity of overconfidence has garnered substantial attention.


**✓ Good**
> Economists study overconfidence because it affects market behavior.

---

## Example: "Shed light on"

**❌ Bad**
> Our findings shed light on the underlying mechanisms.


**✓ Good**
> Our findings show that personal feedback works better than group feedback.

---

## The Principle

**Use direct verbs.**

- Not "embarks on" → "examines", "tests", "investigates"
- Not "sheds light on" → "shows", "demonstrates", "reveals"
- Not "paves the way" → "enables", "allows", "makes possible"

---

# How to Replace Blacklisted Words

---

## Strategy 1: Use Specific Numbers

**❌ Blacklisted**
> We collected a substantial amount of data.


**✓ Specific**
> We collected data from 1,200 households over 10 years.

---

## Strategy 2: Use Clear Descriptions

**❌ Blacklisted**
> We use a sophisticated methodology.


**✓ Clear**
> We use a randomized controlled trial with three treatment arms.

---

## Strategy 3: Use Direct Statements

**❌ Blacklisted**
> It is crucial to note that our findings are robust.


**✓ Direct**
> Our findings hold across five robustness checks.

---

## Strategy 4: Let Data Speak

**❌ Blacklisted**
> The policy had a substantial and significant impact.


**✓ Data-driven**
> The policy reduced unemployment by 1.8 percentage points (p < 0.01).

---

# Practice Exercise: "Find and Replace"

---

## Exercise 1

**Original:**
> Our comprehensive study offers a novel framework and provides robust evidence that the policy was a substantial success.


**Your turn:**
> ?

---

## Exercise 1: Answer

**Original:**
> Our comprehensive study offers a novel framework and provides robust evidence that the policy was a substantial success.


**Better:**
> Our study presents a framework showing that the policy reduced unemployment by 1.8 percentage points.

Or:
> Using panel data from 50 cities, we find that the policy cut unemployment by 1.8 percentage points.

---

## Exercise 2

**Original:**
> This groundbreaking research is vital for understanding the intricate dynamics of the market.


**Your turn:**
> ?

---

## Exercise 2: Answer

**Original:**
> This groundbreaking research is vital for understanding the intricate dynamics of the market.


**Better:**
> This research explains how supply and demand interact in thin markets.

Or:
> This research shows that in markets with fewer than 10 sellers, prices respond slowly to demand shocks.

---

## Exercise 3

**Original:**
> It is interesting to note that various factors seem to play a role.


**Your turn:**
> ?

---

## Exercise 3: Answer

**Original:**
> It is interesting to note that various factors seem to play a role.


**Better:**
> Three factors predict success: prior experience, education, and network size.

---

## Exercise 4

**Original:**
> This paper embarks on a journey to elucidate the profound implications of our findings, which pave the way for future work.


**Your turn:**
> ?

---

## Exercise 4: Answer

**Original:**
> This paper embarks on a journey to elucidate the profound implications of our findings, which pave the way for future work.


**Better:**
> This paper explains how our findings inform policy design.

Or:
> Our findings suggest three directions for future research: testing long-term effects, examining different populations, and exploring alternative feedback mechanisms.

# Real-World Examples

---

## Before: A Typical Abstract

> This comprehensive study embarks on a novel exploration of the intricate determinants of overconfidence. Through a sophisticated experimental design utilizing a substantial sample of participants, we provide robust evidence that personalized feedback interventions can significantly mitigate overconfidence bias. The findings shed light on the nuanced mechanisms underlying this important phenomenon and pave the way for vital contributions to both theory and practice.

---

## After: A Clear Abstract

> We test whether personalized feedback reduces overconfidence. In a lab experiment with 311 subjects, participants estimated their performance on a general knowledge quiz. We randomly assigned subjects to receive either personal feedback (their own score) or group feedback (the average score). Personal feedback reduced overconfidence by 15%; group feedback had no effect. Men reduced their estimates by 2.2 percentage points; women showed no significant change. These results suggest that organizations can use personalized feedback to reduce overconfidence.

---

## What Changed?

1. ~~"comprehensive study embarks on a novel exploration"~~ → "We test whether"
2. ~~"intricate determinants"~~ → specific research question
3. ~~"sophisticated experimental design utilizing a substantial sample"~~ → "lab experiment with 311 subjects"
4. ~~"robust evidence that... can significantly mitigate"~~ → "reduced by 15%"
5. ~~"shed light on the nuanced mechanisms"~~ → specific finding about gender differences
6. ~~"pave the way for vital contributions"~~ → "suggest that organizations should..."

---

# The Core Principle

**Simple, direct, precise, and evidence-based.**

Let data and findings speak for themselves rather than using adjectives to claim importance.

---

# Your Turn: Edit Your Own Work

**Look for these patterns in your writing:**

1. Am I claiming importance instead of showing it?
2. Am I using empty adjectives instead of specific details?
3. Am I being vague when I could be precise?
4. Am I using wordy phrases instead of direct verbs?

---

# Remember

**You are a scholar, not a salesperson.**

Your job is to:
- State your question clearly
- Describe your methods precisely
- Report your findings honestly
- Let the reader judge the importance
