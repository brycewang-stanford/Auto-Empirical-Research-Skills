# Stage 1: Structural Edit

First stage of the multi-stage copy-editing process. Focuses exclusively on high-level structure and argument flow **without** sentence-level editing.

## Purpose

Evaluate and improve:
- Overall argument architecture
- Section organization and sequencing
- Paragraph structure and flow
- Logical coherence and transitions
- Narrative clarity

## Core Philosophy (McCloskey)

**Writing is Thinking Made Visible**
- Structure emerges through writing, not before it
- "You don't learn the details of your argument until speaking or writing it out in detail"
- Content and structure are inseparable

**The Reader Runs the Show**
- "Write not merely so that the reader can understand but so that he cannot possibly misunderstand"
- Clarity = speed directed at the point, without puzzles or distractions
- "If the reader thinks what you write is unclear, then it is"

**Make Writing Hang Together**
- Coherence achieved through repetition and linking, not mechanical transitions
- Transitive pattern: (AB)(BC)(CD) — each sentence links to the previous one
- "The reader can understand writing that hangs together... She can't understand writing filled with irrelevancies"

## Instructions

### Step 1: Read and Understand the Document

**Read the full document** to understand:
- The main argument or thesis
- The paper's structure (introduction, body sections, conclusion)
- The intended audience and field
- The logical flow of ideas

**DO NOT make any edits yet** - this is analysis first.

### Step 2: Analyze Overall Structure

**Check the Introduction**:
- Does it clearly state the research question or problem?
- Does it explain why the question matters?
- Does it preview the main findings or argument?
- Does it provide a roadmap of the paper?

**Check the Body**:
- Are sections in logical order?
- Does each section build on the previous one?
- Are there any missing pieces in the argument?
- Are there redundant sections?

**Check the Conclusion**:
- Does it synthesize the main findings?
- Does it connect back to the introduction?
- Does it avoid just repeating the introduction?
- Does it discuss implications or limitations?

**Check Overall Flow**:
- Can a reader follow the argument from start to finish?
- Are there logical jumps or gaps?
- Is the pacing appropriate (not too rushed, not too slow)?

### Step 2.5: Identify and Eliminate Common Structural Defects

**McCloskey's Anti-Patterns: What to Reject**

Before proceeding to paragraph analysis, scan for these COMMON DEFECTS that undermine readability:

**DEFECT 1: The "Jonesian Arrangement"**
- Pattern: "Say what you're going to say; say it; say that you've said it"
- Why it fails: "Drains the drama from your writing. It is b-o-ring"
- How to fix: State findings directly; don't preview excessively or repeat unnecessarily
- Example of violation: Introduction previews all findings → Body presents findings → Conclusion repeats findings
- Churchill required all proposals on half a sheet of paper to prevent this

**DEFECT 2: Lengthy Overtures (Padding)**
- Pattern: Opening with extensive background before getting to the point
- Why it fails: "Padding, material you collected but is beside the point"
- How to fix: Start with a hook; state your question early; cut the warm-up
- Mark for elimination: Any introduction that takes >3 paragraphs to state the research question

**DEFECT 3: Anticipation Phrases**
- Pattern: "As we shall see," "As I will show later," "More on that in Section 4"
- Why it fails: "Useless and irritating" — breaks flow and condescends
- How to fix: Delete all anticipation phrases; let the structure speak for itself
- Remember: "The reader reads in an hour what took you days to write"

**DEFECT 4: Table-of-Contents Paragraphs**
- Pattern: "The outline of this paper is as follows. Section 2 reviews... Section 3 presents... Section 4 analyzes..."
- Why it fails: "Don't, please, please, for God's sake, don't" — 99 out of 100 readers skip to the substance
- How to fix: Remove entirely; your prose should read well WITHOUT these crutches
- Exception: Very long documents (>50 pages) may need signposting, but make it substantive, not mechanical

**DEFECT 5: Traffic Directions**
- Pattern: "As I said earlier," "Returning to the point made above," "As mentioned in the introduction"
- Why it fails: Clots the prose; makes reading laborious
- How to fix: Remove all backward and forward references; if you must refer back, do so substantively
- Test: If removing the phrase doesn't hurt comprehension, it was unnecessary

**DEFECT 6: Mechanical Linking Words**
- Pattern: Heavy use of "However," "Furthermore," "Indeed," "Therefore," "Nevertheless," "Moreover"
- Why it fails: "English achieves coherence by repetition, not by frantic signaling"
- How to fix: Replace with repetition-based linking (repeat key words from previous sentence)
- ESPECIALLY avoid: "not only... but also" — "It marks you as incompetent. The best writers never use it"

**DEFECT 7: Unjustified Repetition**
- Pattern: Saying the same thing twice without acknowledgment
- Why it fails: Signals you haven't thought through the arrangement
- How to fix: Either eliminate the repetition OR apologize for it ("again," "as noted earlier")
- Red flag: If you find yourself apologizing too much, you're repeating too much — reorganize instead

**ACTION ITEMS for Step 2.5:**

For each defect found:
1. Mark the location (section and paragraph)
2. Classify severity (critical/moderate/minor)
3. Plan the fix (delete/replace/reorganize)

Create a "Defects to Eliminate" list before proceeding to Step 3.

### Step 3: Analyze Paragraph Structure

For each major section, evaluate paragraphs using **McCloskey's Four Elements**:

1. **Topic Sentence**: Does each paragraph start with a clear statement of its main point?
2. **Logical Flow (Transitive Writing)**: Does each sentence connect to the previous one?
3. **Evidence**: Is the claim supported with data, examples, or reasoning?
4. **Climax**: Does the paragraph end with its most important point?

**CRITICAL TESTS for Paragraph Coherence:**

**Test A: The Transitive Pattern (AB)(BC)(CD)**

Every paragraph must create a chain of linked sentences:
- First sentence: Introduces concept A, mentions B
- Second sentence: Starts with B (or pronoun referring to B), introduces C
- Third sentence: Starts with C (or pronoun), develops D
- Continue the pattern throughout

Example of GOOD transitive writing:
```
[Sentence 1: AB] "Economists study inflation using various models."
[Sentence 2: BC] "These models typically rely on historical data."
[Sentence 3: CD] "Historical data, however, may not predict future shocks."
```

Each sentence picks up where the previous left off — creating flow WITHOUT mechanical transitions.

**Test B: The Third Sentence Rule**

"It doesn't much matter what your first sentence is. It doesn't even much matter what the second is. But the third damn well better follow from the first and second."

Action: For every paragraph, read sentences 1-2-3 together. If sentence 3 doesn't connect, diagnose:
- Is sentence 3 a non-sequitur?
- Does it introduce entirely new material without linking?
- Can you add a linking word/phrase from sentence 2 into sentence 3?

**Test C: The Gerald Graff Trick (Last Sentence Simplification)**

"Make the last sentence a simple, street-talk encapsulation of what you've been saying, a sentence Mom could understand instantly."

Why it works:
- "The paragraph can be as technical as you want as long as in the last sentence you come down a notch or two"
- Creates a satisfying climax
- Ensures the paragraph has made a clear point

Action: Check the last sentence of each paragraph:
- Is it simpler than the rest?
- Does it capture the paragraph's essence?
- Could a non-specialist understand it?

If not, add or revise the last sentence to provide a plain-English takeaway.

**Test D: Coherence Through Repetition (Not Mechanical Linking)**

English achieves coherence by repetition of key words, NOT by "However," "Furthermore," "Therefore."

Action for each paragraph:
1. Identify the key concept (usually in topic sentence)
2. Check: Is this key concept (or synonyms/pronouns) repeated in subsequent sentences?
3. If sentences feel disconnected, add repetition of key terms
4. Use pronouns (it, them, these) to relieve monotony while maintaining links

Violation of schoolmarm's rule: "To get transitive writing you must violate the schoolmarm's rule of not repeating words. Verily, you must repeat them, linking the sentences."

**Common paragraph problems to identify**:
- Paragraphs without clear topic sentences
- Broken transitive pattern (sentences don't connect)
- Violation of Third Sentence Rule (sentence 3 doesn't follow from 1-2)
- Missing Gerald Graff ending (no simple final sentence)
- Missing key word repetition (reliance on mechanical transitions instead)
- Missing evidence or unsupported claims
- Weak endings (paragraphs that trail off without climax)
- Paragraphs that are too long (>8-10 sentences) or too short (1-2 sentences)

### Step 4: Identify Structural Issues

Document all structural problems found. **Categorize them**:

**Critical Issues** (must fix):
- Illogical section ordering
- Missing key components (e.g., no clear research question)
- Major argumentative gaps
- Incoherent narrative flow

**Moderate Issues** (should fix):
- Weak transitions between sections
- Redundant content that should be consolidated
- Paragraphs in wrong sections
- Poor paragraph structure (missing elements)

**Minor Issues** (nice to fix):
- Suboptimal section breaks
- Paragraphs that could be reordered for better flow
- Weak topic sentences

### Step 5: Develop Revision Strategy

**Arrangement is Art, Not Formula (McCloskey)**

"Your arrangement should be artful. Make it interesting."

Don't rely on standard academic templates. Think hard about arrangement:
- "Arrangement is like good statistical or historical or detective work, searching the data for patterns"
- There is no one right way to organize a paper
- The best arrangement makes the argument compelling and clear

**For each critical or moderate issue**, determine:

1. **What's the problem?** (Be specific)
2. **Why is it a problem?** (Impact on clarity or argument)
3. **What are possible solutions?** (List 2-3 options if multiple approaches exist)
4. **Which solution is most artful?** (Which makes the argument most compelling?)

**Test Your Proposed Changes:**

Before implementing, mentally test using these methods:

1. **Cold Reading Test**: Imagine reading this a day later with fresh eyes
   - Will the new arrangement still make sense?
   - Are you just moving deck chairs, or truly improving flow?

2. **Rapid Reading Test**: The reader will read in an hour what took you days to write
   - Can the new structure be followed at reading speed?
   - Have you removed obstacles to rapid comprehension?

3. **Graves & Hodge Method**: Note every place where "reading pace is checked by some difficulty of expression"
   - Does your new arrangement eliminate these speed bumps?
   - Have you inadvertently created new ones?

4. **Read It Out Loud** (after implementing): "You'll detect the bad spots"

### Step 6: Present Options for Substantial Changes

**IF** you're considering substantial changes like:
- Reordering major sections
- Removing or combining large chunks of content
- Changing the narrative structure or focus
- Fundamentally reorganizing the argument

**THEN** you must present options to the user before implementing.

**Use this format with AskUserQuestion tool**:

```
🔄 STRUCTURAL REVISION NEEDED

Issue: [Describe the structural problem clearly]

I've identified [2-3] possible approaches:

**Option A**: [Concise description]
Approach: [How it would work]
Pros:
  - [Benefit 1]
  - [Benefit 2]
Cons:
  - [Drawback 1]
  - [Drawback 2]

**Option B**: [Concise description]
Approach: [How it would work]
Pros:
  - [Benefit 1]
  - [Benefit 2]
Cons:
  - [Drawback 1]
  - [Drawback 2]

**Option C** (if applicable): [Concise description]
Approach: [How it would work]
Pros:
  - [Benefit 1]
Cons:
  - [Drawback 1]

📌 RECOMMENDATION: I recommend Option [X] because [clear reasoning based on the paper's goals and evidence].
```

Then use AskUserQuestion to let the user choose.

### Step 7: Implement Structural Changes

After user approval (if needed), implement changes in this order:

**7a. Section-level changes**:
- Reorder sections if needed
- Add section headings if missing
- Combine or split sections as appropriate

**7b. Paragraph-level changes**:
- Move paragraphs to appropriate sections
- Reorder paragraphs within sections for logical flow
- Split overly long paragraphs
- Combine very short paragraphs (if they share a topic)

**7c. Add transitions**:
- Add transition sentences between sections
- Add transition phrases between paragraphs
- Ensure each paragraph connects to the previous one

**7d. Fix paragraph structure using McCloskey's tests**:

For each paragraph that needs improvement:

1. **Add/strengthen topic sentence** (if missing or weak)
   - Make it a clear statement of the paragraph's point
   - Ensure it advances the argument (not just description)

2. **Establish transitive pattern (AB)(BC)(CD)**:
   - Check that sentence 2 picks up from sentence 1
   - Check that sentence 3 follows from sentences 1-2 (Third Sentence Rule)
   - Continue pattern through entire paragraph
   - Add key word repetition where sentences feel disconnected

3. **Apply Gerald Graff Trick to final sentence**:
   - Make it simpler than the rest of the paragraph
   - Ensure it encapsulates the paragraph's point
   - Test: Could a non-specialist understand this sentence?

4. **Ensure evidence supports claims**:
   - Every assertion needs backing (data, examples, reasoning)
   - Don't leave claims floating without support

5. **Remove mechanical transitions**:
   - Replace "However," "Furthermore," "Therefore" with key word repetition
   - Let coherence emerge from content, not signaling

6. **Test for irrelevancies**:
   - Cut anything that doesn't advance this paragraph's topic
   - Every sentence must contribute to the paragraph's point

7. **Verify climax**:
   - Most important point should be at the end (or beginning as second-best)
   - Don't let paragraphs straggle (adding "one more thing" at the last minute)

### Step 8: Document Changes Made

After editing, provide a summary:

```

Section-level:
- [List major section changes]
- [e.g., "Moved literature review before methodology for better flow"]

Paragraph-level:

- [N] paragraphs split (were too long)
- [N] paragraphs combined (were too short)

```

## What NOT to Do in Stage 1

**DON'T** make sentence-level edits yet:
- ❌ Don't fix wordiness
- ❌ Don't change passive to active voice
- ❌ Don't fix grammar or punctuation
- ❌ Don't improve word choice

**WHY?** Because you might move or delete these sentences in structural editing. Wait for Stage 2 (Line Edit) to polish the language.

**DO** focus only on:
- ✅ Argument structure
- ✅ Section organization
- ✅ Paragraph order and structure
- ✅ Logical flow and transitions

## Key Principles

**From McCloskey (Core Philosophy)**:
- "Writing is thinking made visible" - structural problems reflect unclear thinking
- "The reader runs the show" - write so they cannot possibly misunderstand
- Coherence through repetition and linking, not mechanical transitions
- Every paragraph needs four elements: Topic Sentence, Logical Flow, Evidence, Climax

**McCloskey's Transitive Writing**:
- Pattern: (AB)(BC)(CD) — each sentence links to the previous
- Third Sentence Rule: Sentence 3 must follow from sentences 1-2
- Achieve links through key word repetition, not "However"/"Therefore"
- Violate the schoolmarm's rule against repetition to create flow

**McCloskey's Paragraph Techniques**:
- **Gerald Graff Trick**: Final sentence should be simple, street-talk encapsulation
- **One Purpose**: Each paragraph discusses one topic completely
- **Every Paragraph Has a Point**: Tell the reader something new or surprising
- **Avoid Irrelevancies**: Cut anything that doesn't advance the topic

**McCloskey's Anti-Patterns (Eliminate These)**:
- ❌ Jonesian Arrangement ("Say what you'll say, say it, say what you said")
- ❌ Lengthy Overtures (padding before getting to the point)
- ❌ Anticipation ("As we shall see," "More on that later")
- ❌ Table-of-Contents Paragraphs ("Section 2 reviews... Section 3 presents...")
- ❌ Traffic Directions ("As I said earlier," "Returning to the point above")
- ❌ Mechanical Linking ("However," "Furthermore," "Indeed," "not only... but also")
- ❌ Unjustified Repetition (saying same thing twice without acknowledgment)

**McCloskey's Arrangement Philosophy**:
- "Your arrangement should be artful. Make it interesting"
- "Arrangement is like detective work, searching the data for patterns"
- No single right way to organize — think hard, don't follow formulas
- Test with cold reading, rapid reading, Graves & Hodge method

**From Thomson**:
- Make the paper scannable - readers should be able to grasp the structure by skimming
- Don't bury the lead - state main findings early
- Create a narrative arc, not just a list of findings

**From Strunk & White**:
- Rule 22: Place emphatic words at the end (applies to paragraphs too)
- Rule 19: Express coordinate ideas in similar form (parallel structure for lists)

## Common Structural Problems

**Problem 1: Jonesian Arrangement (The Triple-Say)**
- Introduction previews all findings
- Body presents the findings
- Conclusion repeats the findings
- **Why it's bad**: "Drains the drama from your writing. It is b-o-ring"
- **Fix**: State findings once, substantively. Make introduction and conclusion do different work.

**Problem 2: Table-of-Contents Paragraphs**
- "The outline of this paper is as follows. Section 2 reviews... Section 3..."
- **Why it's bad**: 99 out of 100 readers skip to the substance
- **Fix**: Remove entirely. Your prose should be clear WITHOUT this crutch.

**Problem 3: Traffic Direction Overload**
- Heavy use of "as we shall see," "as I said earlier," "more on that later"
- **Why it's bad**: Clots the prose; makes reading laborious
- **Fix**: Delete all anticipation and backward references. Let structure speak for itself.

**Problem 4: Mechanical Linking Addiction**
- Paragraphs start with "However," "Furthermore," "Indeed," "Therefore," "Nevertheless"
- **Why it's bad**: English achieves coherence through repetition, not frantic signaling
- **Fix**: Replace with key word repetition from previous sentence. Create transitive flow.

**Problem 5: Inverted Pyramid (Common in Drafts)**
- Introduction buries the research question
- Main findings appear only in conclusion
- **Fix**: State research question early; preview findings in introduction

**Problem 6: Lengthy Overtures (Padding)**
- Introduction takes >3 paragraphs to state the research question
- Extensive background before getting to the point
- **Why it's bad**: "Padding, material you collected but is beside the point"
- **Fix**: Start with a hook; cut the warm-up; state question in paragraph 1 or 2

**Problem 7: Chronological Organization (Research Order)**
- Paper follows the order in which research was conducted
- Not the order that makes sense to readers
- **Fix**: Reorganize to follow logical argument order (arrangement is art!)

**Problem 8: Section Imbalance**
- One section is 10 pages, another is 2 paragraphs
- Suggests unclear priorities
- **Fix**: Expand short sections or consolidate into others

**Problem 9: Broken Transitive Pattern**
- Each paragraph is self-contained
- Sentences within paragraphs don't connect (AB)(BC)(CD)
- Third sentence doesn't follow from first two
- **Fix**: Add key word repetition; create sentence-to-sentence links; apply Third Sentence Rule

**Problem 10: Missing Gerald Graff Endings**
- Paragraphs end with complex, technical sentences
- No simple encapsulation of the point
- **Fix**: Make last sentence simpler, street-talk summary of paragraph

**Problem 11: Missing Roadmap (Different from Table-of-Contents)**
- Reader doesn't know the argument's trajectory
- No substantive signposting
- **Fix**: Add substantive preview in introduction (not mechanical "Section 2 reviews...")

## Example: Before and After Structural Edit

### Example 1: Document-Level Structure

**BEFORE** (Poor Structure):
```
Introduction: [Vague background, no clear question stated]
"This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews... Section 3 presents..."

Section 2: Results [jumps right to results without setup]

Section 3: Methods [methods appear after results]

Section 4: Literature Review [appears too late]

Conclusion: [Just repeats introduction word-for-word]
```

**AFTER** (Good Structure):
```
Introduction:
- Clear research question (paragraph 1)
- Why it matters (paragraph 2)
- Preview of findings (paragraph 3)
- [NO table-of-contents paragraph]

Section 2: Literature Review
- Narrative leading to research gap
- Positions this paper as next logical step

Section 3: Methods
- How we address the research question
- Data and approach

Section 4: Results
- Main findings with clear interpretation

Section 5: Discussion
- Implications of findings
- Limitations and future research
- Broader significance

Conclusion:
- Synthesis (not repetition)
- Take-home message
```

### Example 2: Paragraph-Level (Applying McCloskey's Principles)

**BEFORE** (Multiple Defects):
```
However, monetary policy has important effects on inflation. Furthermore, central
banks must consider employment. Indeed, the dual mandate is challenging. As we shall
see in Section 4, interest rate adjustments are the primary tool. Not only do central
banks use conventional policy, but also they employ unconventional measures during
crises.
```

**Problems**:
- Starts with "However" (mechanical linking)
- Uses "Furthermore," "Indeed" (mechanical transitions)
- Uses "As we shall see" (anticipation)
- Uses "not only... but also" (marks you as incompetent)
- No transitive pattern (sentences don't connect)
- No clear topic sentence
- No climax (ends weakly)

**AFTER** (McCloskey's Principles Applied):
```
Central banks face a dual mandate: controlling inflation while maintaining employment.
This mandate creates a fundamental challenge. The challenge becomes acute during economic
crises, when conventional interest rate adjustments prove insufficient. Unconventional
measures then become necessary—but they're a poor substitute for good design.
```

**What Changed**:
✅ Clear topic sentence (dual mandate)
✅ Transitive pattern:
  - Sentence 1 (AB): Central banks → dual mandate
  - Sentence 2 (BC): mandate → challenge
  - Sentence 3 (CD): challenge → crises → conventional tools insufficient
  - Sentence 4 (DE): unconventional measures necessary
✅ Key word repetition creates flow ("mandate," "challenge")
✅ Gerald Graff ending: simple, clear takeaway
✅ Eliminated all mechanical transitions
✅ Eliminated anticipation ("as we shall see")
✅ Emphatic words at end: "poor substitute for good design"

### Example 3: Fixing Broken Transitive Pattern

**BEFORE** (Third Sentence Rule Violation):
```
Markets respond to uncertainty with volatility. Risk aversion increases during
financial crises. Diversification is an important investment strategy.
```

**Problem**: Sentence 3 doesn't follow from sentences 1-2 (fails Third Sentence Rule)

**AFTER** (Transitive Pattern Restored):
```
Markets respond to uncertainty with volatility. This volatility reflects increased
risk aversion, particularly during financial crises. Risk aversion, in turn, drives
investors toward diversification strategies.
```

**What Changed**:
✅ Sentence 2 picks up "volatility" from sentence 1
✅ Sentence 3 picks up "risk aversion" from sentence 2
✅ Creates clear chain: uncertainty → volatility → risk aversion → diversification
✅ Each sentence flows naturally from the previous

## Tips for Success

**Before You Begin:**
1. **Read the whole document first** before making any changes
2. **Identify the core argument** - can you state it in 2-3 sentences?
3. **Think about the reader's journey** - what do they need to know and when?
4. **Think hard about arrangement** - "like detective work, searching for patterns"

**During Structural Analysis:**
5. **Scan for the Seven Defects** (Step 2.5) - eliminate anti-patterns first
6. **Apply the Four Tests** to every paragraph:
   - Test A: Transitive pattern (AB)(BC)(CD)
   - Test B: Third Sentence Rule
   - Test C: Gerald Graff ending
   - Test D: Coherence through repetition
7. **Check for narrative flow** - does each section naturally lead to the next?
8. **Look for redundancy** - is anything said twice without acknowledgment?
9. **Verify completeness** - are there gaps in the argument?

**Testing Your Changes:**
10. **Cold reading**: Will this make sense with fresh eyes tomorrow?
11. **Rapid reading**: Can it be read in an hour without confusion?
12. **Graves & Hodge method**: Note where reading pace is checked
13. **Read out loud**: "You'll detect the bad spots"

**Final Checks:**
14. **Test paragraph isolation** - read each paragraph alone; does it make sense?
15. **Test paragraph sequence** - does each link to the previous?
16. **Verify emphasis** - are most important points at ends of paragraphs/sections?
17. **Check for irrelevancies** - cut anything that doesn't advance the argument

## Quick Reference: McCloskey's Structural Editing Checklist

Use this checklist when implementing structural changes:

### Document Level
- [ ] Introduction states research question early (paragraphs 1-2)
- [ ] Introduction previews findings substantively (not mechanically)
- [ ] **NO table-of-contents paragraph** ("Section 2 reviews... Section 3...")
- [ ] Conclusion synthesizes (doesn't repeat introduction)
- [ ] Arrangement is artful and interesting (not formulaic)
- [ ] Sections ordered logically (not chronologically by research order)

### Paragraph Level
- [ ] Each paragraph has clear topic sentence
- [ ] **Transitive pattern maintained**: (AB)(BC)(CD)
- [ ] **Third Sentence Rule**: Sentence 3 follows from sentences 1-2
- [ ] **Gerald Graff ending**: Final sentence is simple, clear takeaway
- [ ] Key words repeated for coherence (not mechanical "However," "Furthermore")
- [ ] Each paragraph makes a point (tells reader something new)
- [ ] Most important point at end of paragraph (climax)
- [ ] Evidence supports all claims
- [ ] No irrelevancies (every sentence advances topic)

### Sentence Level (for structural stage)
- [ ] Key word repetition creates links between sentences
- [ ] No mechanical transitions: "However," "Furthermore," "Indeed," "Therefore," "Nevertheless"
- [ ] **NEVER use "not only... but also"**
- [ ] No anticipation: "As we shall see," "More on that later"
- [ ] No traffic directions: "As I said earlier," "Returning to the point above"
- [ ] No unjustified repetition (if you repeat, acknowledge with "again")

### Anti-Patterns Eliminated
- [ ] ❌ Jonesian Arrangement (say-say-say)
- [ ] ❌ Lengthy overtures (>3 paragraphs before research question)
- [ ] ❌ Table-of-contents paragraphs
- [ ] ❌ Anticipation phrases
