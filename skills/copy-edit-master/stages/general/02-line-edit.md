# Stage 2: Line Edit + Proofread

Second and final stage of the copy-editing process. Applies all style rules at the sentence level plus grammar, punctuation, and spelling corrections.

## Purpose

Apply systematic improvements to:

**Line Editing:**
- Word choice and concision (eliminate wordiness)
- Voice (active vs. passive)
- Specificity (concrete vs. vague language)
- Tense consistency
- Sentence rhythm and variety
- Emphasis and word order
- Natural flow and coherence

**Proofreading:**
- Grammar (subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference)
- Punctuation (commas, semicolons, periods)
- Spelling and typos
- Capitalization
- Citation formatting consistency

### Step 2: Apply Rule 17 - Omit Needless Words (THE GOLDEN RULE)

**"A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences."** — Strunk & White

**"If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out."** — George Orwell

This is the most important rule. Go through the document systematically and eliminate wordiness.

**2a. Hunt for empty phrases** - Delete or replace:

| ❌ Wordy | ✅ Concise |
|---------|----------|
| the question as to whether | whether |
| there is no doubt that | clearly / doubtless |
| due to the fact that | because / since |
| at this point in time | now |
| in the event that | if |
| for the purpose of | to / for |
| in order to | to |
| it is important to note that | [delete] |
| it is worth noting that | [delete] |
| it should be emphasized that | [delete] |
| the fact that | [delete or one word] |
| the existence of | [delete] |

**2b. Academic-specific wordy phrases** - Delete or replace:

| ❌ Wordy Academic | ✅ Concise |
|------------------|----------|
| this paper aims to explore | [just state it] |
| the purpose of this study is to | We examine / We test |
| our analysis suggests that | We find |
| the results indicate that | The results show |
| it is possible that | may / might |
| in the context of | in |
| with respect to | about / regarding |
| with regard to | about / regarding |
| a number of | many / several / [specific number] |
| a wide range of | various / [list them] |
| in most cases | usually |
| not only...but also | [rewrite more simply] |

**2c. McCloskey's "Eschew Surplusage"**:

Delete weak adverbs and most adjectives:
- **very** → delete (almost always)
- **absolutely, purely, totally** → delete (most things aren't)
- **interesting, significant** (as adjectives) → delete and let facts speak
- **for convenience** → delete (all writing should be convenient)

**2d. Apply the core principle**:

- This doesn't mean make all sentences short
- It means make every word count
- If a word can be removed without loss of meaning, remove it
- Sydney Smith: "Run your pen through every other word you have written; you have no idea what vigour it will give"

**Increment your counters** each time you eliminate wordiness or delete unnecessary adjectives/adverbs.

### Step 3: Apply Rule 16 - Use Definite, Specific, Concrete Language

**"Prefer the specific to the general, the definite to the vague, the concrete to the abstract."** — Strunk & White

**"Au Bon Pain baguette > bread > widgets > X"** — McCloskey (singulars beat plurals, concrete beats abstract)

**3a. Replace vague adjectives with exact numbers**:

| ❌ Vague | ✅ Specific |
|---------|-----------|
| substantial increase | 12% increase |
| large sample | sample of 4,237 firms |
| significant effect | β = 0.18 (p = 0.02) |
| strong correlation | r = 0.72 (p < 0.001) |
| many observations | 15,432 observations |
| recent years | 2015-2020 |
| high proportion | 73% of respondents |

**3b. Name things specifically**:

| ❌ Vague | ✅ Specific |
|---------|-----------|
| various factors | income, education, and age |
| several methods | OLS, IV, and propensity score matching |
| different approaches | qualitative interviews and archival analysis |
| multiple specifications | fixed effects, random effects, and pooled OLS |

**3c. McCloskey's Concrete Examples Principle**:

Replace abstract categories with concrete examples:

| ❌ Abstract | ✅ Concrete |
|-----------|-----------|
| natural resource-oriented exports | sheep and wheat |
| the commencement of the Spanish Price Revolution antedated the inflow of treasure | Spanish prices began to rise before treasure came |
| geographical and cultural factors function to spatially confine growth | It's a good bet that once a place gets poor it will stay poor |

**3d. Apply the principle**: If you measured it, report the measurement. If you can name them, name them. Concrete examples translate up to principles easier than abstractions translate down.

**Increment your counter** for each vague→specific replacement.

### Step 4: Apply Rule 15 - Put Statements in Positive Form

Make definite assertions. State what IS, not what IS NOT.

**4a. Replace negative constructions**:

| ❌ Negative/Hedging | ✅ Positive |
|--------------------|------------|
| The coefficient is not insignificant | The coefficient is significant (β = 0.18, p = 0.02) |
| The results do not contradict our hypothesis | The results support our hypothesis |
| We cannot rule out the possibility that | Our evidence suggests that |
| It is not uncommon | It is common / occurs frequently |
| did not consider | ignored / overlooked |

**4b. Avoid excessive hedging**:

Don't stack qualifiers: ~~may possibly suggest that it could potentially~~

Choose ONE hedge:
- ❌ "may possibly" → ✅ "may"
- ❌ "could potentially" → ✅ "could"
- ❌ "might suggest that could" → ✅ choose one

**4c. When to hedge (appropriately)**:
- Genuine uncertainty: "This pattern may reflect selection bias"
- Interpretation: "These findings suggest (not prove) that..."
- Speculation: "Future research could test whether..."

**4d. When NOT to hedge**:
- When reporting your actual results: "We find that X increases Y by 15%"

**Exception**: Negative form IS appropriate for null results:
- ✅ "We find no evidence that the placebo treatment affects outcomes"

### Step 5: Apply Rule 14 - Use the Active Voice + McCloskey's "Use Active Verbs"

**"Find the action and express it in a verb."** — McCloskey

Replace passive constructions with active voice where appropriate. More importantly, express actions as verbs, not nouns.

**5a. Convert passive to active**:

| ❌ Passive | ✅ Active |
|-----------|----------|
| It was found that | We find |
| The data were analyzed | We analyze the data |
| It is shown in Table 2 | Table 2 shows |
| It is argued that | We argue / Smith (2019) argues |
| The hypothesis was tested | We tested the hypothesis |
| The results were obtained | We obtained the results |

**5b. Find buried verbs (de-nominalization)**:

Circle every "is" in your writing — many hide buried verbs in nouns.

| ❌ Nominalization (Weak) | ✅ Active Verb (Strong) |
|-------------------------|------------------------|
| There is a data reanalysis need | We must reanalyze our data |
| Self-interested exchange is a knowledge-generating process | Self-interested exchange generates knowledge |
| The model is a representation of | The model represents |
| This provides an explanation for | This explains |
| The study gives consideration to | The study considers |

**5c. When passive IS appropriate**:
- Agent is irrelevant: "The model is estimated using GMM"
- Technical descriptions: "Data were collected from 10-K filings"
- Conventional phrasing: "The null hypothesis is not rejected"

**5d. Mixed voice strategy** (best practice):
- Use ACTIVE for your choices and actions: "We estimate," "We test," "We find"
- Use PASSIVE for technical procedures where agent is obvious: "estimated using OLS"

**McCloskey's principle: "Verbs make English."** Active, accurate, lively verbs create active, accurate, lively prose.

**Increment your counter** for each passive→active conversion and buried verb discovery.

### Step 6: Apply Rule 20 - Keep Related Words Together

Fix modifier placement and avoid separating related elements.

**6a. Fix "only" placement**:

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---------|----------|
| We only tested three models | We tested only three models |
| The policy only affects large firms | The policy affects only large firms |

Place "only" immediately before the word it modifies.

**6b. Don't separate subject and verb** with long phrases:

❌ "The coefficient, after controlling for firm fixed effects, industry trends, and macroeconomic shocks, remains positive"

✅ "After controlling for firm fixed effects, industry trends, and macroeconomic shocks, the coefficient remains positive"

**6c. Fix dangling modifiers**:

❌ "Using propensity score matching, the treatment effect was estimated"

✅ "Using propensity score matching, we estimate the treatment effect"

**6d. Check modifier clarity**:

Every modifier should clearly attach to what it modifies. Test by asking: "Does the modifier placement change meaning?"

### Step 7: Apply Rule 19 - Express Coordinate Ideas in Similar Form (Parallelism)

**"Express coordinate ideas in parallel form."** — Strunk & White (and McCloskey agrees)

Use parallel construction for lists and series.

**7a. Fix list parallelism**:

❌ "First, we identify the effect; second, the role of Z is documented; third, we show implications"

✅ "First, we identify the effect. Second, we document the role of Z. Third, we show the implications"

**7b. Fix verb form parallelism**:

❌ "The model allows us to test three predictions: first, identifying peer effects; second, measuring spillovers; third, distinguishing selection from influence"

✅ "The model allows us to test three predictions: first, to identify peer effects; second, to measure spillovers; third, to distinguish selection from influence"

**7c. Use parallel structure with correlatives**:
- both X and Y (not both X and also Y)
- either X or Y (not either X or also Y)
- not only X but also Y (not not only X but Y as well) — **But better: rewrite to avoid "not only...but also" entirely (see Step 2b)**

**7d. Test for parallelism**:

Read lists aloud. Your ear will catch non-parallel structures.

### Step 8: Apply Rule 22 - Place Emphatic Words at the End + McCloskey's Emphasis Architecture

**"The emphatic words of a sentence should come at the end."** — Strunk & White

**"The end is the place of emphasis. The reader leaves the sentence with the last word ringing in her mental ears."** — McCloskey

This step combines Strunk & White's Rule 22 with McCloskey's extensive guidance on natural emphasis through structure.

#### 8a. Fix Weak Endings

Put the most important information at the end of sentences and paragraphs.

❌ "CEO overconfidence matters for corporate decisions, according to our findings"

✅ "Our findings show CEO overconfidence matters for corporate decisions"

❌ "The treatment effect is statistically significant at the 1% level in most specifications"

✅ "In most specifications, the treatment effect is significant at the 1% level"

#### 8b. Don't Waste the Ending On:
- "in our study"
- "among others"
- "for further research"
- "respectively" (also signals poor parallelism — fix that instead)

#### 8c. DELETE All Fake Emphasis Words

**McCloskey's emphatic rule: NEVER announce importance — create it through structure.**

Delete these phrases completely:
- ❌ "It is important to note that..."
- ❌ "It should be emphasized that..."
- ❌ "It is noteworthy that..."
- ❌ "Significantly," (as sentence starter)
- ❌ "Interestingly," (as sentence starter)
- ❌ "Fortunately," (as sentence starter)

These are "cheap ways of introducing irrelevant opinion" — they admit the prose itself lacks emphasis.

**Instead, create emphasis naturally through:**
1. **Position** (end of sentence, end of paragraph)
2. **Brevity** (after long sentences, a short one jolts)
3. **Concrete detail** (specificity emphasizes)
4. **Active verbs** (action emphasizes)
5. **Repetition of key terms** (coherence emphasizes what matters)

#### 8d. The Architecture of Emphasis

**End = Primary Emphasis**:
- Examine every sentence: Does the main idea come at the end?
- Example transformation:
  - ❌ "The end of the sentence is the emphatic location"
  - ✅ "The end is the place of emphasis"
  - (The rewrite puts emphasis on *emphasis* — the key idea — not on *location*)

**Beginning = Secondary Emphasis**:
- Start strong or end strong — preferably end
- Dump less important things in the middle, or in the trash

#### 8e. Gerald Graff's Paragraph Trick

**"Make the last sentence a simple, street-talk encapsulation of what you've been saying."**

The paragraph can be as technical as you want — but the last sentence brings it home in language your mother could understand instantly.

Example:
- Technical paragraph about regression discontinuity design...
- Last sentence: **"In short, the policy changed behavior."**

This is positioning for emphasis.

#### 8f. Line Up Series by Increasing Length

Put lengthy bits at the end:
- ✅ "faith, hope, and charity" (works — escalates)
- ❌ "charity, faith, and hope" (doesn't work)
- ✅ "Keynes and the Keynesians" (works)
- ❌ "The Keynesians and Keynes" (doesn't work)

**Note**: This may conflict with putting the most important matter at the end. Be aware and choose deliberately.

#### 8g. Avoid Straggling Sentences

Like the foolish young man of Japan whose limericks never would scan, avoid sentences that keep adding "one more thing" at the end, which causes straggling, which makes the sentence hard to read, which will cause the reader to stop reading.

Fix by:
- Cutting the extra bits
- Moving them to the beginning
- Making them separate sentences

### Step 9: Apply McCloskey's Additional Principles

Beyond the overlap with Strunk & White, McCloskey offers crucial additional guidance.

#### 9a. Delete Adjectives and Adverbs Ruthlessly

**"Adjectives and adverbs should be first to go in revision."** — McCloskey

**"Nouns and especially verbs carry the weight."**

Hunt for and delete:

| ❌ Delete | ✅ Keep (if essential) | Reason |
|----------|----------------------|--------|
| very | [delete] | Almost never needed |
| really | [delete] | Weak intensifier |
| quite | [delete] | Vague |
| rather | [delete] | Vague |
| somewhat | [delete] | Vague |
| fairly | [delete] | Vague |
| extremely | [delete] | Hyperbolic |
| highly | [delete] | Vague |
| absolutely | [delete] | Most things aren't |
| purely | [delete] | Most things aren't |
| clearly | [delete or keep sparingly] | Often unnecessary |

**Sydney Smith's rule**: "Run your pen through every other word you have written; you have no idea what vigour it will give"

Start with -ly words. Most should go.

**Increment your "Adjectives/adverbs deleted" counter**.

#### 9b. Be Plain, Not Fancy — McCloskey's Bad Words List

**"Avoid Latinate abstraction. Use Anglo-Saxon and Norman French words."**

Replace pompous words with simple ones:

**Vague Nouns to Replace:**

| ❌ Pompous/Vague | ✅ Plain/Specific |
|-----------------|------------------|
| concept | idea, notion, thought |
| process | [strike it: "transition process" → "transition"] |
| structure | [be specific: what kind?] |
| situation | [be specific: what situation?] |
| function | role, purpose |
| individuals | people |
| agents | people, firms, traders |
| methodology | method |
| framework | [be specific or delete] |

**Pretentious Verbs to Replace:**

| ❌ Pretentious | ✅ Simple |
|---------------|----------|
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help, enable |
| implement | do, carry out |
| finalize | finish, complete |
| commence | begin, start |
| terminate | end, stop |
| hypothesize | suppose, expect |
| state (meaning "say") | say |
| critique (verb) | criticize |

**Clumsy Prepositional Phrases:**

| ❌ Clumsy | ✅ Simple |
|----------|----------|
| subsequent to | after |
| prior to | before |
| in close proximity to | near |
| in the event that | if |
| in terms of | about, for |
| with respect to | about |
| with regard to | about |
| for the purpose of | for, to |

**Traffic Signals to Use Sparingly:**

Overuse of these marks incompetence:
- thus, hence (use sparingly)
- not only...but also (rewrite entirely)
- former/latter (requires reader to look back — never make readers solve puzzles)

**Increment your "Pompous → Plain" counter** for each replacement.

#### 9c. Avoid Elegant Variation — Use One Word to Mean One Thing

**"Use one word to mean one thing. Repeat the same word for clarity, not synonyms for 'elegance'."** — McCloskey

**Ms. Jones was wrong: Repetition is the soul of clarity in English.**

❌ Bad (elegant variation):
"Democratic institutions are crucial. On the other hand, the autocratic system has different properties. The authoritarian regime..."

✅ Good (consistent repetition):
"Democratic institutions are crucial. Autocratic institutions have different properties. Autocratic institutions..."

**Or even better (use pronouns for repetition relief):**
"Democratic institutions are crucial. Autocratic institutions have different properties. They..."

**Key principle**:
- In academic writing, technical terms should be REPEATED, not varied
- "Democratic" means one thing — don't swap in "participatory," "representative," "liberal" unless they mean different things
- Repetition creates coherence; variation creates confusion

**Increment your "Elegant variation fixed" counter** when you standardize terminology.

#### 9d. Make Writing Cohere Through Repetition

**"Transitive writing: Each sentence links to the previous via repeated words."** — McCloskey

**"English achieves coherence by repetition, not by frantic signaling."**

Fix:
❌ "The regression shows a significant effect. However, this result depends on the specification."

Better — repeat for coherence:
✅ "The regression shows a significant effect. This effect depends on the specification."

Even better — use pronouns:
✅ "The regression shows a significant effect. It depends on the specification."

**Avoid clumsy traffic signals**:
- however, thus, furthermore, moreover, consequently

**Use them sparingly**. Instead, link through:
- Repeated nouns
- Pronouns (it, them, this X)
- Repeated roots (economy → economic → economically)

**Test**: Can you follow the chain of repeated words through a paragraph? If not, fix it.

**Increment your "Coherence improvements" counter**.

#### 9e. Create Sentence Rhythm and Variety

**"Prose has rhythms, some better than others."** — McCloskey

**Avoid monotony**: If every sentence is the same length and construction, the paragraph becomes monotonous.

**Vary sentence length naturally**:
- **Short sentences punctuate.** (Like this.)
- Medium sentences develop ideas and provide most information.
- Long sentences, with their clauses and qualifications, allow you to express complex relationships and show how ideas connect to each other.

**Example of good rhythm:**
"We test three hypotheses. First, we examine whether peer effects matter. We find strong evidence that they do, with a coefficient of 0.42 (p < 0.001). This effect persists across all specifications."

(Note the variation: short, medium, medium-long, short)

**John Gardner's Three-Part Sentence Principle**:
- English sentences have three parts: subject, verb, object
- **Vary which part you elaborate** — choose only ONE per sentence
- **Don't overburden all three parts** — it exhausts the reader

Example (elaborate verb only):
"An English sentence [simple subject] / has, grammatically speaking, [elaborate verb] / three parts [simple object]."

**Listen for rhythm**: Read aloud. Ugly rhythms reveal themselves to the ear.

**Increment your "Rhythm adjustments" counter** when you vary sentence length or structure.

#### 9f. Tense Consistency

**Choose tense deliberately and stick to it.**

**Use PRESENT TENSE for:**
- Your findings: "We find that X affects Y"
- What your tables/figures show: "Table 2 shows..."
- Your argument: "We argue that..."
- What your model predicts: "The model predicts..."
- Timeless truths: "Markets clear"

**Use PAST TENSE only for:**
- Specific historical events: "The crisis occurred in 2008"
- What other researchers did: "Smith (2019) found..."
- Your completed procedures: "We recruited 200 subjects"

**Check the entire document for tense consistency**. Don't mix tenses unnecessarily.

❌ Inconsistent:
"We found that X affects Y. Table 2 showed the results."

✅ Consistent:
"We find that X affects Y. Table 2 shows the results."

**Increment your "Tense corrections" counter**.

#### 9g. Subject Consistency

**Choose ONE research subject and stick to it:**

Options:
- **"We"** (most common in academic writing, even single-authored papers)
- **"I"** (acceptable for single-authored papers, less common)
- **DO NOT MIX** "we," "the authors," "this paper," "the study," and passive voice

❌ Inconsistent:
"This paper examines X. We find that Y. The authors argue that Z."

✅ Consistent:
"We examine X. We find that Y. We argue that Z."

**Avoid "this paper" and "this study" as subjects** — they're weak. Papers don't examine things; researchers do.

#### 9h. Read Aloud — The Ear Test

**"Read out loud. The ear test reveals bad spots your eye misses."** — McCloskey

- Don't write anything you'd be embarrassed to read aloud to the intended audience
- Detect unintentional rhymes, pompous sentences, jargony words
- Train your ear by reading the best: Shakespeare, good journalism, poetry

**Test every paragraph**:
- Does it flow when spoken?
- Do you stumble anywhere?
- Does anything sound pompous or awkward?

If yes → fix it.

### Step 10: Final Sweep - Hunt for Common Patterns

Use pattern matching to catch remaining issues.

**10a. Check all instances of "this," "that," "these," "those"**:

**McCloskey: "Avoid this, that, these, those — the plague of modern prose."**

- Vague demonstratives are weak
- They point backward, interrupting forward flow
- Replace with plain "the" or repeat the noun

❌ "This shows that..."
✅ "This finding shows that..." OR "This result shows that..."

**Better yet:**
✅ "The regression shows that..."

**10b. Check hedging balance**:
- Not too much: don't stack (may, might, could, possibly, potentially)
- Not too little: acknowledge genuine uncertainty

**10c. Check abstract endings**:
- Abstract should end with most concrete, specific finding
- ❌ "These findings have important implications"
- ✅ "We find that X increases Y by 23%, suggesting..."

**10d. Delete all boilerplate**:

Never write:
- ❌ "This paper aims to..."
- ❌ "The outline of this paper is as follows..."
- ❌ "As we will see..."
- ❌ "In conclusion, we have shown that..." (just state the conclusion)

**10e. Check for clumsy sentences with frantic signaling**:

- Overuse of "however," "thus," "therefore," "moreover"
- Replace with repetition and pronouns for coherence

**10f. Fix "gapping" inconsistencies**:

Choose whether to repeat words in parallel constructions:
- "the beautiful and damned" OR "the beautiful and the damned"
- Be consistent within your document

### Step 11: Proofread

After completing line edits, perform comprehensive proofreading:

**11a. Grammar checks**:
- Subject-verb agreement
- Pronoun reference clarity
- Verb tense consistency (already checked in Step 9f, but verify)
- Parallel structure (already checked in Step 7, but verify)
- Complete sentences (no unintentional fragments)

**11b. Punctuation checks**:
- Comma placement (especially around "however," "therefore," "thus")
  - McCloskey: "By ear, not by rule; delete most commas before 'the'"
- **Semicolon (;)**: Means "likewise" or "also" — marks parallel thoughts
- **Colon (:)**: Means "to be specific" — introduces illustrations
- **Oxford comma**: Always use: "faith, hope, and charity"
- Quotation marks (check placement relative to punctuation)
- Hyphens vs. en-dashes vs. em-dashes
- **Avoid comma splice**: Don't connect two sentences with just a comma

**11c. Spelling and typos**:
- Run spell-check
- Check for common academic typos (affect/effect, principle/principal)
- Check for homophone errors (their/there/they're, its/it's)

**11d. Capitalization**:
- Section headings consistent
- Proper nouns capitalized
- Check title case vs. sentence case consistency

**11e. Citation formatting**:
- Consistent citation style throughout
- All citations have corresponding references
- Check for missing parentheses, dates, page numbers

## What NOT to Do in Stage 2

**DON'T** make structural changes:
- ❌ Don't reorder sections or paragraphs
- ❌ Don't split or merge paragraphs
- ❌ Don't add or remove major content
- ❌ Don't change the argument structure

That was Stage 1's job. Stage 2 focuses ONLY on sentence-level edits within each paragraph. Paragraph positions are locked.

## Key Principles Reference

### Strunk & White's Rules (Core 7)
- **Rule 17**: Omit needless words (THE GOLDEN RULE)
- **Rule 16**: Use definite, specific, concrete language
- **Rule 15**: Put statements in positive form
- **Rule 14**: Use the active voice
- **Rule 20**: Keep related words together
- **Rule 19**: Express coordinate ideas in similar form
- **Rule 22**: Place emphatic words at the end

### McCloskey's Principles (Integrated)
1. **Use active verbs** — find the action, express it in a verb
2. **Delete adjectives and adverbs** — nouns and verbs carry weight
3. **Be concrete, not abstract** — singulars beat plurals, examples beat categories
4. **Be plain, not fancy** — Anglo-Saxon beats Latinate
5. **Avoid elegant variation** — repeat key terms for clarity
6. **Create coherence through repetition** — not through traffic signals
7. **Read out loud** — the ear test catches what the eye misses
8. **Use parallel form** — readers expect patterns
9. **Position emphasis at the end** — last word rings in mental ears
10. **Use complete sentences** — fragments confuse
11. **Vary sentence length for rhythm** — avoid monotony
12. **Delete fake emphasis words** — create emphasis through structure
13. **Be consistent in tense** — present for findings, past for history
14. **Be consistent in subject** — choose "we" or "I" and stick with it

### The Ultimate Rules

**George Orwell's Six Rules:**
1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active
5. Never use a foreign phrase, scientific word, or jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous

**Mark Twain's Seven Rules:**
1. Say what you are proposing to say, not merely come near it
2. Use the right word, not its second cousin
3. Eschew surplusage
4. Not omit necessary details
5. Avoid slovenliness of form
6. Use good grammar
7. Employ a simple and straightforward style

## Common Patterns to Find and Fix

Use Grep to find these patterns, then fix them:

**Wordiness patterns:**
- "the fact that" → delete or rephrase
- "it is [adjective] that" → delete "it is... that"
- "there is/are" → rephrase to eliminate
- "in order to" → "to"
- "due to the fact that" → "because"
- "the existence of" → delete

**Passive voice patterns:**
- "was/were [verb]ed" → active voice
- "it was found/shown/argued" → "we find/show/argue"

**Vague language patterns:**
- "significant" without numbers → add exact statistics
- "substantial," "considerable," "large" → replace with numbers
- "various," "several," "multiple" → list them or give number
- "concept," "process," "structure" → be specific

**Pompous language:**
- "utilize" → "use"
- "facilitate" → "help"
- "methodology" → "method"
- "individuals" → "people"
- "prior to" → "before"
- "subsequent to" → "after"

**Hedging stacks:**
- "may possibly" → "may"
- "could potentially" → "could"
- "might suggest that could" → choose one qualifier

**Fake emphasis:**
- "It is important to note that" → delete
- "It should be emphasized that" → delete
- "Interestingly," "Significantly," "Fortunately," → delete

**This-isms:**
- "This shows" → "This result shows" OR "The regression shows"
- "These are" → "These findings are" OR repeat the noun

## Note

1. **Work paragraph by paragraph with context** - process one paragraph at a time, using prev/next paragraph context for transitions
2. **DO NOT move paragraphs** - positions are locked from Stage 1
3. **Use Find/Replace** for systematic patterns within paragraph (e.g., "the fact that")
4. **Read aloud** - your ear catches awkwardness (McCloskey's ear test)
5. **Count as you go** - maintain the statistics throughout
6. **Every word must earn its place** - ruthless concision

## Example: Before and After Line Edit

**BEFORE** (Wordy, vague, passive, pompous):
```
It was found that there was a significant relationship between CEO overconfidence and firm
investment decisions. The results indicate that, in the context of high uncertainty, the
effect is substantially larger. This finding is interesting and suggests that overconfident
CEOs may potentially be more likely to make investment errors due to the fact that they
overestimate their ability to predict future outcomes. This concept is important for
understanding corporate behavior.
```

**AFTER** (Concise, specific, active, plain):
```
We find that CEO overconfidence increases firm investment by 18% (p < 0.01). In high-
uncertainty periods, this effect triples to 54% (p < 0.001). Overconfident CEOs make
larger investment errors because they overestimate their ability to predict future
outcomes.
```

**Changes made**:
- "It was found" → "We find" (Rule 14: active voice)
- "significant relationship" → "increases by 18%" (Rule 16: specific)
- "substantially larger" → "triples to 54%" (Rule 16: specific)
- "This finding is interesting and suggests that" → deleted (fake emphasis words)
- "may potentially be more likely" → eliminated (hedging stack)
- "due to the fact that" → "because" (Rule 17: concise)
- "This concept is important" → deleted entirely (fake emphasis + vague)
- Deleted: "in the context of" → "in" (McCloskey: plain)
- Word count: 84 words → 43 words (49% reduction)

## The Reader is Always Right

**"Write not merely so that the reader can understand but so that he cannot possibly misunderstand."** — Quintilian (via McCloskey)

- If a reader thinks it's unclear, it is — quit arguing
- Respect your reader: don't waste their time with puzzles or boilerplate
- Every word matters; every sentence should advance understanding
- Clarity is both a practical and an ethical matter
